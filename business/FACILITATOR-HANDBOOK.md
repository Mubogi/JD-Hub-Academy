# Facilitator Handbook

**JD Hub Academy** · Mubogi Gastavas Jordan, President, JD Hub Tech · First Edition, 2026

This handbook is for anyone delivering a JD Hub Academy course, whether as the
owner, an associate facilitator, or a licensed partner institution. It is the
difference between delivering our curriculum and delivering a course that merely
uses our books.

---

## 1. The non-negotiables

Five rules. Break them and it is not our course.

**1. Every session has a lab.** No session is a lecture. If you talked for two
hours, you taught nothing.

**2. Every lab uses the learner's own work.** Not a sample business, not a
fictional bakery. Their real business, their real job, their real assignment.

**3. You demonstrate live, on screen, before you explain.** Curiosity first.
Theory second. Never slides-first.

**4. You show the AI failing on purpose, in every course that uses AI.** A learner
who has never seen a confident wrong answer will trust the wrong thing.

**5. Every session ends with one sentence from each learner: "Tomorrow I will…"**
If they cannot finish the sentence, the session did not land.

---

## 2. The standard session shape

Two hours. Adjust proportionally for shorter sessions.

| Time | Segment | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Recap | One learner shows last week's homework. Answer three questions fast. |
| 0:10–0:30 | Teach | Live demo (60 seconds), then the why in three points maximum. |
| 0:30–1:30 | Lab | Everyone on their own task. You roam. Timer visible on screen. |
| 1:30–1:50 | Share | Two volunteers show screens. Critique the *prompt*, never the person. |
| 1:50–2:00 | Brief | Homework. One sentence each: "Tomorrow I will…" |

**Roaming rules.** Four minutes maximum at any one learner. If three learners hit
the same wall, stop the room and fix it on the big screen. The room is your
signal, not the individual.

## 3. Managing the three learner types

You will always have all three in the room. Train them differently.

### The frightened

They believe they will break something, or be replaced. They will not ask
questions in front of others.

**What they need:** safety first. Tell them explicitly what not to do, and tell
them their judgement is still the product. Pair them with a patient neighbour.
Check on them quietly during labs.

**What not to do:** single them out, or move too fast in front of the room.

### The curious

They will experiment and get good results, inconsistently. They are your engine
for the share segment.

**What they need:** structure. Give them R-T-C-F, the follow-up moves, and the
prompt library. They will fill it in themselves.

### The impatient

They want the shortcut and will disengage from anything abstract.

**What they need:** one task, done live, that saves them an hour this week. Give
them that in the first twenty minutes and they are yours for the course. Give them
theory first and you have lost them.

## 4. The five things that go wrong

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Wi-Fi dies | Mobile hotspot. Pre-downloaded apps. Fallback: learners who got it working teach the others — which is better teaching anyway. |
| A learner cannot create an account (no card, no number) | Pre-create demo accounts. Pair them. Use the web version on a shared laptop. |
| One learner races ahead and gets bored | Make them the roaming helper for fifteen minutes. Teaching cements it and they stay engaged. |
| Learners copy prompts without understanding | Critique the prompt in every share segment. Ask "why did you add that line?" |
| Someone wants to argue about AI taking jobs | Acknowledge honestly in sixty seconds, redirect to "what will you be the person who can do?", then back to the lab. Do not debate. |

## 5. Assessment

Every course book contains its rubric. Use it as printed. Do not invent your own
weightings.

**The rules:**

- The practical component is mandatory regardless of total score. A learner who
  cannot demonstrate the work does not pass, however good their written answers.
- Score the artefact, not the effort.
- Give the rubric to learners at the start, not the end. They should know what
  excellent looks like from day one.
- Return scores within one week. Late feedback teaches nothing.

**What a certificate must state:** learner name, course title, contact hours,
tools covered, capstone title, date, facilitator signature. Specificity is what
makes it credible to an employer.

## 6. Handling the difficult conversations

### "This is too fast for me."

Never slow the room. Slow the individual. Pair them, give them the recording, and
offer fifteen minutes before the next session. Slowing the room punishes everyone
else and does not solve their problem.

### "I already know this."

Ask them to demonstrate it on their own work, in front of you. Either they do, in
which case make them a helper, or they do not, in which case the demonstration
taught them more than you could have.

### "AI is dangerous / the devil / stealing jobs."

Take it seriously. Acknowledge the real concerns — job displacement, deepfakes,
cheating, data misuse — in sixty seconds, without defensiveness. Then say: "The
question I can help with is what you personally will be able to do." Return to the
lab. You are not there to win an argument.

### "Can you just do it for me?"

No. Sit beside them and ask them to click. The whole value is that they can do it
without you next week.

## 7. Preparing for a cohort

**Two weeks before:**

- [ ] Confirm the venue, test the Wi-Fi, confirm the power supply
- [ ] Confirm enrolment numbers; cancel if below break-even (7 for most courses)
- [ ] Send the pre-course form: "What is the one task you most want AI to do for you?"
- [ ] Read the answers. Use them as your examples throughout the course.

**One week before:**

- [ ] Print the prompt cards and assessment sheets
- [ ] Check current free-tier limits for every tool you will teach
- [ ] Prepare demo accounts and two spare devices
- [ ] Send the joining instructions: date, time, venue, what to bring, what to install

**The day before:**

- [ ] Test the projector with your own laptop
- [ ] Charge every device
- [ ] Download offline copies of anything you need
- [ ] Read your own session plan once

## 8. Using the course books

Every learner receives the book for their course. The books are designed to be
used *during* the sessions, not read afterwards.

- Point learners to the exercise they are doing, in the book.
- Use the prompt boxes directly — they are copy-ready.
- The Scripture callouts are for reflection, not preaching. Read them, pause, move
  on. Do not turn a business course into a sermon.
- The capstone rubric is printed in the book. Use it as printed.

**What facilitators may do:** print the book for their cohort, quote up to 200
words with attribution, adapt exercises to their local context.

**What facilitators may not do:** rebrand the material, remove the author's name,
sell it separately, or deliver it as their own course.

## 9. Licensed partner institutions

A licensed partner may deliver any course in the catalogue to their own students,
under their own brand, with our books and rubrics, for an annual fee.

**Included:** the books, the rubrics, the facilitator handbook, and one training
day for their facilitators per year.

**Required:** named facilitators trained by us, the rubric used as printed, and a
short report at the end of each cohort (numbers enrolled, completed, passed).

**Why institutions license:** it is faster and cheaper than building a curriculum,
and it comes with a certificate that means something because the standard is
enforced.

Contact: JD Hub Academy, JD Hub Tech, Kampala.

## 10. The standard we are actually holding

Anyone can teach a tool. The thing we are actually teaching is judgement: when to
trust the output, when to check it, when to refuse it, and how to tell the
difference.

A learner who leaves able to generate impressive text but unable to spot a
confident error is a liability we created. A learner who leaves cautious, capable
and honest is a professional.

That is the standard. Hold it.
