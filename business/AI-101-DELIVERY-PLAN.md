# AI-101 — Session Delivery Plan

The teaching script for **AI in Everyday Life & Business**. Twelve sessions,
two hours each. Every session names the book section, the live demo, the lab
task, and the exit test that tells you the room actually got it.

Read alongside the [Facilitator Handbook](FACILITATOR-HANDBOOK.md), which covers
the standard session shape and how to handle the three learner types. This
document is the specific content for this course.

---

## Before session one

Three things decide whether the cohort finishes.

**1. Everyone is on the same tool.** Do not let the room split across four
assistants in session one. Pick one — ChatGPT free is the default — and run the
first four sessions on it. Introduce Gemini and Meta AI in sessions five and six,
once the prompting habit is set. Learners who meet three tools in week one learn
none of them.

**2. Everyone has an account before they arrive.** Send this message at
enrolment:

> Before the first session, create a free ChatGPT account at chat.openai.com
> using the email address you check daily. If you cannot, arrive 20 minutes
> early and we will do it together.

Twenty minutes of account creation in session one destroys the session. It
happens every cohort if you do not pre-empt it.

**3. Every learner brings one real task from their own work.** Not a hypothetical.
A real invoice, a real product to sell, a real email they are dreading writing.
The whole course runs on their material, not on samples. Collect these in the
enrolment form and you will know your room before you meet it.

## Session 1 — What AI actually is

**Book:** Module 1, Lessons 1.1–1.4 · **Exercise 1.4**

**Opening move (10 min).** Ask the room: "What is the difference between a
calculator and ChatGPT?" Take three answers. Someone will say "ChatGPT can
think." Write that on the board and leave it there. You will come back to it.

**The 60-second demo.** Type: *"Who was the president of Uganda in 1875?"* Let it
answer confidently. Then ask it to cite a source. Watch it produce something that
does not exist. Say nothing for five seconds. The silence does more than any
explanation.

**Teach (20 min).** It predicts the next word. That is the whole mechanism. It
does not look anything up unless you tell it to. Three consequences:

| Failure | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | A confident, specific, invented fact | Ask for a source, then check it |
| Bias | Generic, Western, male-default answers | Give it your context explicitly |
| Leakage | You pasted client data into a public tool | Never paste what you cannot publish |

**Lab (40 min).** Exercise 1.4 — Meet the machine. Learners ask it five things
about their own work and mark each answer *trust*, *check*, or *never*. Walk the
room. The moment someone finds their first confident wrong answer, stop the room
and let them show it. That is the lesson landing.

**Close (10 min).** Return to the board. Erase "ChatGPT can think." Replace it
with "ChatGPT can predict." Ask what changed. Homework: bring one task from work
that you would like to hand over.

**Exit test.** Each learner states one thing AI cannot do. If they say "it cannot
feel", push back — that is philosophy, not practice. You want "it cannot know
what it has not been told" or "it cannot check itself."

## Session 2 — Prompting that works

**Book:** Module 2, Lessons 2.1–2.4 · **Exercises 2.3, 2.4**

**Opening move (10 min).** Show two prompts on screen — the weak and the strong
example from Lesson 2.1. Run both live. Let the room see the gap before you
explain it.

**Teach (20 min).** R-T-C-F. Role, Task, Context, Format. Do not rush this.
Write it on the board and leave it there for the rest of the course.

The line that lands with business audiences: *"If you can brief a member of
staff, you can brief a model — you just have to be more explicit, because it
cannot see the room and will not tell you it is confused."*

**Lab (40 min).** Exercise 2.3 — the three-round improvement. Everyone writes a
weak prompt, runs it, adds one R-T-C-F element, runs it again, adds another, runs
it again. Three outputs side by side on their own screen. The improvement is
visible and it is theirs.

Then Exercise 2.4 — speak a prompt. Use the voice button. This matters more than
it sounds: most learners type more stiffly than they speak, and their spoken
prompts are better.

**Close (10 min).** Two volunteers read their before-and-after. Critique the
prompt, never the person.

**Exit test.** Every learner can name the four elements without looking.

## Session 3 — ChatGPT in daily work

**Book:** Module 3, Lessons 3.1–3.3

**Teach (20 min).** The five work patterns — draft, summarise, rewrite, analyse,
plan. One verb each. Learners stop asking "what can it do" and start asking
"which pattern is this task."

**Lab (40 min).** Everyone takes the real task they brought from work and runs it
through all five patterns. Not five exercises — one task, five angles. This is
where the course stops being about AI and starts being about their job.

**Exit test.** A learner can say which of the five patterns their task needed and
why.

---

## Session 4 — Files and custom instructions

**Book:** Module 3, Lesson 3.4 · **Exercise 3.4**

**Why this session exists.** Session 3 makes them productive. Session 4 makes
them fast. The standing brief is the single highest-return thing in the course.

**Teach (15 min).** Upload a real PDF and ask questions about it. Then show what
happens when you ask about something not in the document — it guesses. Lesson 3.3
in the book.

**Lab (45 min).** Exercise 3.4 — build your standing brief. Four lines: who you
are, what you do, who you serve, how you want answers. Paste into custom
instructions. Start a fresh conversation and ask a normal work question. Compare.

The room goes quiet here. That is the sound of people getting their time back.

**Exit test.** Each learner has a working standing brief and can show a before
and after.

## Session 5 — Gemini in the inbox

**Book:** Module 4, Lessons 4.1–4.4 · **Exercise 4.4**

**Setup.** Learners need a Gmail account. Most have one. The few who do not pair
with someone who does.

**The demo that sells the course.** Open a real 40-email thread. Ask Gemini to
summarise it and list the decisions and the open questions. Thirty seconds.
Then ask the room how long that would have taken them. Let them answer.

**Lab (40 min).** Exercise 4.4 — the email thread test. Each learner uses their
own messy thread. Then Lesson 4.4: knowing what it can see. Have them check which
emails Gemini actually read. Some will be surprised, and that surprise is the
privacy lesson.

**Caution to state plainly.** Gemini in Workspace sees your mail. That is the
feature and the risk. Never let a learner point it at a shared inbox with client
data without their employer's permission.

**Exit test.** A learner can say what Gemini can and cannot see in their account.

## Session 6 — Meta AI and social

**Book:** Module 5, Lessons 5.1–5.4 · **Exercise 5.4**

**Why this session matters commercially.** Most of your learners run a WhatsApp
Business account. Meta AI sits inside the app they already use. This is the
session they tell other people about.

**Teach (15 min).** Reply bank, then captions. Lesson 5.2 and 5.3.

**Lab (40 min).** Exercise 5.4 — build your reply bank. Twenty common customer
questions, twenty drafted replies, saved where they can reach them. By the end
every learner has a customer-service asset they did not have at 9am.

Then captions that do not read as generated. The test: would you post this under
your own name?

**Exit test.** A learner has a saved reply bank of at least ten entries.

## Session 7 — Canva without a designer

**Book:** Module 6, Lessons 6.1–6.4 · **Exercise 6.4**

**The four rules.** One idea per piece. One focal point. Two fonts maximum.
Contrast decides legibility. That is most of design for non-designers.

**The 30-minute poster sprint.** Timer on the screen. Real product, real price,
real phone number, real deadline. No lorem ipsum. Ship or do not ship.

**Lab (45 min).** Exercise 6.4 — the real poster. Then Magic Studio, Lesson 6.3:
which tool does which job, so they stop guessing. Then export, Lesson 6.4 —
correct size, correct format, no stretched logos.

**Exit test.** A finished poster, exported, on their phone, ready to send.

---

## Session 8 — The content engine

**Book:** Module 7, Lessons 7.1–7.3 · **Exercise 7.3**

**Teach (15 min).** The engine in five steps, then the repurposing multiplier.
One long piece becomes seven short ones.

**Lab (45 min).** Exercise 7.3 — schedule a week. Learners leave with seven
posts scheduled, not seven ideas. The difference is the whole course.

**Exit test.** Seven posts, scheduled, with dates.

---

## Session 9 — Numbers and admin

**Book:** Module 8, Lessons 8.1–8.4 · **Exercise 8.4**

**Teach (15 min).** Plain English to formula. Then the warning: AI is confident
about arithmetic and sometimes wrong. Always check the total against a calculator.

**Lab (45 min).** Exercise 8.4 — notebook to dashboard. Take a real notebook page
of figures, get it into a sheet, get a summary. Learners who run a business will
use this the same week.

**Exit test.** A working sheet with a correct total, verified by hand.

---

## Session 10 — Service, research and automation

**Book:** Modules 9, 10, 11 · **Exercises 9.3, 10.3, 11.3**

Three modules in one session. Move fast, lab hard.

**Service (20 min).** Build a bot from your own content, then break it —
Exercise 9.3. The breaking is the lesson. Ask it something it should not answer
and watch what it does.

**Research (25 min).** Exercise 10.3 — the adversarial briefing. Give it a claim
and ask it to argue the other side, with sources. Then check the sources. Some
will not exist. That is the verification ritual in one exercise.

**Automation (25 min).** Exercise 11.3 — one automation, live. Trigger, action,
result. Then the rule: automate what you do weekly and can describe in one
sentence. Nothing else.

**Exit test.** A learner can name one thing they will stop doing by hand.

---

## Session 11 — Ethics and your AI policy

**Book:** Module 12, Lessons 12.1–12.4 · **Exercise 12.4**

**The data rule, stated once and clearly.** If you would not put it on a public
noticeboard, do not paste it into a public AI tool. Everything else in this
session follows from that.

**Lab (45 min).** Exercise 12.4 — write and test the policy. One page. Who may
use AI, for what, with what data, who checks the output, what happens when it
goes wrong. Then test it against three scenarios from their own work.

Learners who employ staff should leave with a policy they can actually issue.
That is a deliverable, not an essay.

**Exit test.** A one-page policy, tested against three scenarios.

---

## Session 12 — Capstone

**Book:** Modules 13 and 14 · **Exercise 13.3**

**The brief.** Build one workflow you will still be using in ninety days. It must
save measurable time, use at least three things from the course, and be
demonstrable in five minutes.

**The rubric** is printed in the book, Module 13, Lesson 13.2. Use it as printed.
Do not invent your own on the day.

**The room.** Eight to twelve presentations, five minutes each, three minutes of
questions. Invite one outsider — an employer, a former learner, a local business
owner. It changes the quality of what gets presented.

**Close with Module 14.** The habit that makes it stick: one hour, same time each
week, on your own work. And where to go next — AI-201 for those who want to build.

**Certificates** are issued on passing the rubric, not on attendance. Say this at
enrolment and again in session one, so nobody is surprised.

## What to watch for

| Symptom | What it usually means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Room goes silent in the lab | They are working, not stuck | Leave them alone |
| One learner is far ahead | They are bored, not gifted | Give them the next module's exercise |
| Three learners hit the same wall | Your demo was unclear | Stop the room, fix it on screen |
| Nobody asks questions | They are lost and hiding it | Ask one person directly, gently |
| Attendance drops at session 5 | The course got abstract | Bring back a real task immediately |

---

## Between cohorts

**Keep three things.** The best capstone from each cohort, with permission. The
questions learners asked that the book did not answer. The one exercise that
consistently failed, and why.

**Fix one thing per cohort.** Not five. A course that improves once every eight
weeks is a course that is still good in three years.

**Ask for the referral at session 12, not later.** "Who else do you know who
needs this?" asked in the room, while the capstone glow is still on them, works
ten times better than an email a week later.
