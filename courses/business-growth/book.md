# Module 1 — The Difference Between a Hustle and a Business

A hustle depends on the person doing it. A business does not. That is the whole
distinction, and everything in this course follows from it.

If you stop working for a week and the money stops, you have a hustle. That is not
a moral failing — every business starts as a hustle, and a hustle is how you find
out whether anyone wants what you have. But a hustle cannot grow past the hours in
your day, cannot be sold, cannot be left to your family, and cannot survive you
being ill.

::: scripture
"Through wisdom a house is built, and by understanding it is established; by
knowledge the rooms are filled with all precious and pleasant riches."
— Proverbs 24:3–4
:::

Built by wisdom, established by understanding, filled by knowledge. Three
different things, in that order. Most people try to skip to filling the rooms.

## Lesson 1.1 — The four stages

| Stage | Signature | Your job |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hustle** | You do everything; income is unpredictable | Find something people pay for |
| **2. Operator** | You do the core work; some help | Document what works |
| **3. Systemiser** | Systems do the work; you manage exceptions | Build the systems |
| **4. Owner** | The business runs; you decide direction | Choose where to go next |

Most businesses die at the transition from 2 to 3, because the founder is too busy
doing the work to document it, and the work is what pays for everything. Breaking
that loop is the central problem of this course.

## Lesson 1.2 — The five numbers

Every business, in every sector, lives or dies on five numbers. If you cannot
state all five from memory, you are flying blind.

**1. Gross margin.** Revenue minus direct cost, as a percentage. A 20 percent
margin business cannot be saved by more sales — it needs a price change.

**2. Monthly fixed costs.** Rent, salaries, subscriptions. The number you must
cover before profit.

**3. Break-even.** Fixed costs ÷ gross margin. The revenue at which you make
nothing.

**4. Cash runway.** Cash in the bank ÷ monthly net burn. How many months you have.

**5. Customer acquisition cost versus lifetime value.** What a customer costs you
to get, against what they are worth.

::: box
**Worked example.**

Revenue 2,000,000. Direct costs 1,400,000. Gross profit 600,000. Gross margin
30 percent.

Fixed costs 450,000. Break-even = 450,000 ÷ 0.30 = 1,500,000.

If revenue drops below 1,500,000, the business is losing money. That single
number tells you what you must sell every month, and it is the number most owners
have never calculated.
:::

## Lesson 1.3 — Why profitable businesses run out of cash

Profit is an accounting idea. Cash is a survival idea. They are not the same.

You sell on credit: profit recorded, no cash. You buy stock for a big order: cash
gone, profit later. You grow fast: more stock, more staff, more receivables, all
paid for before the customer pays you.

The three habits that prevent this: invoice immediately, chase at seven days not
thirty, and keep one month of fixed costs untouched at all times.

::: exercise
**Exercise 1.3 — Calculate your five numbers**

1. From last month's records, calculate gross margin.
2. List your monthly fixed costs.
3. Calculate break-even revenue.
4. Calculate your cash runway in months.
5. Estimate customer acquisition cost and lifetime value.

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** five numbers on one page. If you cannot
find the data, that is your first finding, and it is a serious one.
:::

# Module 2 — Pricing That Protects You

## Lesson 2.1 — Three ways to price

**Cost-plus.** Cost plus a margin. Simple, safe, and it leaves money on the table
when your work is worth more.

**Market rate.** What competitors charge. Easy to justify, and it caps you at
your competitors' ambition.

**Value-based.** A fraction of the value the customer receives. Highest return,
requires you to understand the customer's economics.

Most small businesses should use cost-plus as a floor, market rate as a check, and
value as the target.

## Lesson 2.2 — The margin trap

The most common fatal error: pricing to win work. A job at 8 percent margin
consumes your capacity and produces almost nothing. Two of those and you have
worked a month for free, and you cannot take the good job that arrives next week.

The rule: know your floor. Write it down. Never go below it, no matter how
attractive the order looks.

## Lesson 2.3 — Raising prices without losing customers

Do it in this order:

1. **Add value first.** A faster delivery, a guarantee, a small extra.
2. **Give notice.** Thirty days, in writing, warmly.
3. **Explain the reason honestly.** Costs rose, or the service improved.
4. **Offer a transition.** Existing customers keep the old price for one cycle.
5. **Accept the losses.** You will lose some customers, and your profit will
   still rise.

::: box
**The arithmetic of a price rise.**

Revenue 2,000,000, margin 30 percent, profit 600,000 after fixed costs.

Raise prices 10 percent. If you lose 5 percent of customers, revenue is
2,090,000, and because costs fell slightly with the lost volume, profit rises
meaningfully. In most small businesses, a 10 percent price rise with a 5 percent
customer loss increases profit by more than 20 percent.

Price is the highest-leverage number you control. Most owners never touch it.
:::

::: exercise
**Exercise 2.3 — Set your floor and plan a rise**

1. Calculate your true cost per unit or per job, including your own time at a
   real wage.
2. Set your floor price and write it where you will see it.
3. Identify three customers currently below the floor.
4. Write the price-rise message you will send them.
5. Send it to one this week.

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** a written floor and one sent message.
:::

# Module 3 — The Sales Process

## Lesson 3.1 — Sales is a process, not a personality

If your sales depend on your charm, you cannot delegate them, and you cannot
grow. Charm does not scale; process does.

The five stages, and what must happen at each:

| Stage | Goal | Asset you need |
|---|---|---|
| **Enquiry** | Respond fast and completely | Quick replies, price list |
| **Qualify** | Find out if they can and will buy | Three questions |
| **Propose** | Make the decision easy | A one-page offer |
| **Close** | Ask, and handle the real objection | Objection answers |
| **Follow-up** | Recover the ones who went quiet | A three-touch sequence |

## Lesson 3.2 — The three qualifying questions

Ask these before you spend time on a proposal. They save hours.

1. **What are you trying to achieve, and by when?** (Urgency and outcome.)
2. **What have you tried already?** (Awareness and budget signal.)
3. **Who else is involved in the decision?** (The real decision-maker.)

A prospect who cannot answer the first question is not ready to buy, and a
proposal written for them is wasted work.

## Lesson 3.3 — Handling objections without arguing

The structure: **acknowledge, reframe, prove, ask.**

- "It is too expensive." → "I understand — it is a real investment. Compared to
  what? … Here is what two other customers got for the same amount. Does that
  change how you see it?"
- "I need to think about it." → "Of course. What specifically would you like to
  think through? I may be able to answer it now."
- "I will get back to you." → "Fine. Shall I check in on Thursday, or would
  Monday suit better?" (Always get a specific date.)

Never argue. An argument won is a customer lost.

## Lesson 3.4 — Documenting the process

Write your sales process as a checklist a new employee could follow. This is the
single most valuable document in a small business, and almost nobody has one.

::: exercise
**Exercise 3.4 — Write the checklist**

1. Write each of the five stages as a numbered checklist.
2. Include the exact words for the first message and the three qualifying
   questions.
3. Write answers to your five most common objections.
4. Have someone else follow the checklist on a real enquiry while you watch.

**Time:** 50 minutes. **Deliverable:** a checklist another person can execute.
:::

# Module 4 — Customers Worth Keeping

## Lesson 4.1 — The economics of retention

Acquiring a new customer costs several times more than selling again to an
existing one. Yet most small businesses spend everything on acquisition and
nothing on retention.

Three numbers to track: repeat purchase rate, average time between purchases, and
the percentage of revenue from returning customers. If the last number is below
40 percent, your growth is a leaking bucket.

## Lesson 4.2 — The retention system

Five habits, in order of return:

1. **Acknowledge the purchase.** A message within a day. Costs nothing.
2. **Deliver reliably.** Reliability is more powerful than delight.
3. **Check in after delivery.** One question: "Did it work as expected?"
4. **Remind at the right time.** When a consumable is likely to run out.
5. **Ask for a referral at the moment of satisfaction.** Not later. Then.

## Lesson 4.3 — Referrals, systematically

A referral is the cheapest customer you will ever acquire. Most businesses ask for
referrals randomly, if at all.

Make it a step in the process: at the moment a customer expresses satisfaction,
ask one specific question — "Is there one other person you think would find this
useful?" Specific asks get specific answers.

::: exercise
**Exercise 4.3 — Build the retention sequence**

1. Write the five habits as messages you will actually send.
2. Put them in your calendar or automation.
3. Run them for ten customers this month.
4. Record repeat purchases and referrals.
5. Compare to the previous month.

**Time:** 40 minutes plus one month. **Deliverable:** a measured retention result.
:::

# Module 5 — Systems and Delegation

## Lesson 5.1 — The process document

A process is documented when someone who has never done it can complete it
correctly using only the document.

That is a high bar, and most "process documents" fail it. The test: hand it to a
new person and watch. Every question they ask is a gap.

The one-page format:

::: box
**PROCESS — Preparing a customer quotation**

**Trigger:** Customer requests a price.

**Steps**
1. Collect: item, quantity, delivery location, deadline.
2. Check stock in the sheet. If unavailable, note the lead time.
3. Open the quotation template. Fill the fields.
4. Calculate the total in the sheet. Never in your head or by AI.
5. Send within 4 working hours.
6. Log in the enquiry tracker with a follow-up date.

**Quality check:** total matches the sheet; delivery date is achievable; customer
name spelled correctly.

**Owner:** Sales assistant. **Escalate to:** Owner, if the order exceeds 2,000,000.
:::

## Lesson 5.2 — What to delegate first

Delegate in this order:

1. **Repetitive and rule-based.** Data entry, invoicing, scheduling.
2. **Repetitive and judgement-light.** Standard replies, routine orders.
3. **Skilled and documented.** Anything with a written process.
4. **Judgement-heavy.** Only after someone has earned it.

Never delegate something you have not documented. That is not delegation; it is
abandonment.

## Lesson 5.3 — The delegation conversation

Four sentences:

1. "Here is the outcome I need, and by when."
2. "Here is the process document."
3. "Here is how much you can decide without me, and when to escalate."
4. "Here is when we will review it together."

The third sentence is the one most owners forget, and its absence is why staff
either ask about everything or commit to too much.

::: exercise
**Exercise 5.3 — Document and delegate one process**

1. Choose the process you do most often.
2. Write it in the one-page format.
3. Hand it to someone else and watch them do it. Note every question.
4. Fix the document, not the person.
5. Repeat until they complete it without asking.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** one process that runs without you.
:::

# Module 6 — The Weekly Operating Rhythm

## Lesson 6.1 — Why rhythm beats effort

Businesses fail from inconsistency more than from lack of effort. A weekly rhythm
makes the important things happen every week, regardless of how busy you are.

## Lesson 6.2 — The 90-minute weekly review

Same time every week. Ninety minutes. Four sections.

**Minutes 0–20: Numbers.** Revenue, cash, receivables, pipeline. Compare to last
week and to the same week last year.

**Minutes 20–50: Customers.** Every open enquiry, every late delivery, every
complaint. Decide the action for each.

**Minutes 50–75: Operations.** What broke this week? What will break next? One
process improvement.

**Minutes 75–90: Priorities.** The three things that must happen next week. Three,
not ten.

## Lesson 6.3 — The monthly and quarterly layers

**Monthly:** full accounts, margin by product or service, staff performance,
stock or capacity review.

**Quarterly:** pricing review, customer concentration check, one strategic
question. (What would we stop doing if we had to cut 20 percent of the work?)

::: exercise
**Exercise 6.2 — Institute the rhythm**

1. Book the 90-minute slot, recurring, for the next twelve weeks.
2. Build the one-page review sheet with the four sections.
3. Run it this week, even if the data is incomplete.
4. Note what data you were missing and fix that first.

**Time:** 30 minutes to set up, 90 minutes weekly.
**Deliverable:** four completed review sheets after one month.
:::

# Module 7 — Growth Decisions

## Lesson 7.1 — The three growth routes

**More of the same.** More customers, same product. Simplest, and it hits capacity
first.

**More per customer.** Higher price, larger orders, or additional services. Usually
the most profitable route, and the least used.

**New markets or products.** Highest potential, highest risk, and usually
premature before the first two are exhausted.

## Lesson 7.2 — The capacity question

Before growing, answer: what is the actual constraint? Staff hours, cash, stock,
premises, or your own attention?

Growth that does not address the constraint produces stress, not profit. A
restaurant that fills every seat but cannot serve them loses customers permanently.

## Lesson 7.3 — When not to grow

Do not grow when: margin is below 20 percent, cash runway is under two months,
customer concentration is above 40 percent in one client, or the process is not
yet documented.

Fix the underlying weakness first. Growth amplifies whatever you already are — if
you are chaotic, you become chaotically larger.

::: warn
**The growth trap.** Taking a large order you cannot finance is the most common
way a profitable small business dies. The customer pays in 60 days; your suppliers
want payment in 7. Check the cash requirement before you accept the order, not
after.
:::

::: exercise
**Exercise 7.3 — Name your constraint**

1. Identify your binding constraint right now: staff, cash, stock, space, attention.
2. Write what growth would look like if that constraint were removed.
3. Write what breaks if you grow without removing it.
4. Decide: grow, hold, or fix. Write one sentence and date it.

**Time:** 35 minutes. **Deliverable:** a written decision with a date.
:::

# Module 8 — Using AI in the Business

## Lesson 8.1 — Where it pays

| Function | AI use | Typical gain |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Draft replies, quotes, follow-ups | Response time |
| Marketing | Content, ads, images | Production cost |
| Admin | Invoicing, reports, summaries | Hours per week |
| Analysis | Campaign and sales analysis | Decision speed |
| Training | Onboarding documents, SOPs | Setup time |

## Lesson 8.2 — Where it does not

AI cannot tell you what to sell, what to charge, or whether to take a customer.
Those are judgements with consequences, and it has no stake in the outcome.

It also cannot fix a broken process. Automating a bad process produces bad
results faster.

## Lesson 8.3 — The one workflow to build first

For most small businesses, the highest-return AI workflow is: **enquiry to
quotation in under ten minutes.**

Customer messages → AI drafts the quotation from your price list → you check the
numbers → send. This single workflow typically cuts quote turnaround from hours to
minutes, and quote speed is one of the strongest predictors of winning the work.

::: exercise
**Exercise 8.3 — Build the quote workflow**

1. Put your price list in a document.
2. Write a prompt that takes a customer enquiry and drafts the quotation.
3. Add a hard rule: "Do not calculate totals; use the figures I provide."
4. Run it on ten real enquiries. Time yourself.
5. Compare to your previous method.

**Time:** 50 minutes. **Deliverable:** a measured before-and-after.
:::

# Module 9 — Capstone: The One-Page Growth Plan

## Lesson 9.1 — The brief

One page. Six sections. Written so a lender or an investor could read it in five
minutes.

1. **What the business does**, in two sentences.
2. **The five numbers**, current.
3. **The constraint**, named.
4. **The 90-day plan**, three priorities with dates.
5. **The cash requirement**, and where it comes from.
6. **The risk**, and what you will do if it happens.

## Lesson 9.2 — The rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numbers | 30% | All five, accurate, used in reasoning | Estimates, no margin |
| Constraint | 25% | Correctly identified, plan addresses it | Grows everything at once |
| Plan | 20% | Three priorities, dated, achievable | Ten priorities, no dates |
| Cash | 15% | Requirement calculated, source named | Ignored |
| Risk | 10% | Named, with a response | "Nothing will go wrong" |

::: scripture
"For which of you, intending to build a tower, does not sit down first and count
the cost, whether he has enough to finish it — lest, after he has laid the
foundation, and is not able to finish, all who see it begin to mock him, saying,
'This man began to build and was not able to finish'?"
— Luke 14:28–30
:::

The verse is not about pessimism. It is about finishing. A plan that accounts for
cost is a plan that gets completed, and a completed modest business beats an
abandoned ambitious one every time.
