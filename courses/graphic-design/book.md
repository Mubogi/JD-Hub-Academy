# Module 1 — What Design Actually Does

Design is not decoration. It is the arrangement of information so that a person
knows what to do next. A poster that is beautiful but confusing has failed. A
poster that is plain but instantly clear has succeeded.

This matters commercially. In a market where every stall looks the same, the one
with a legible sign, a consistent colour and a clear price is the one that gets
the customer — not because it is prettier, but because it is easier to trust.

::: scripture
"Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might."
— Ecclesiastes 9:10
:::

Half-finished design is visible from across the street. It is one of the few
crafts where the effort, or its absence, is immediately obvious to everyone.

## Lesson 1.1 — The four principles

**Hierarchy.** The most important thing is biggest. Then the second. Then the
rest. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.

**Contrast.** Things that differ should differ clearly. A slightly different
shade reads as a mistake, not a decision.

**Alignment.** Everything lines up with something. One invisible grid holds the
piece together.

**Space.** Empty space is not wasted. It is what makes the filled space legible.

## Lesson 1.2 — Why amateur design looks amateur

Not because of skill. Because of five specific habits:

1. Too many fonts.
2. Too many colours.
3. Everything centred and slightly misaligned.
4. Text too close to the edges.
5. No clear focal point.

Every one of those is fixable in ten minutes once you can see it.

## Lesson 1.3 — The three-metre test

Print it, or step back from the screen. Can you read the headline from three
metres? Can you tell what it is for? Can you tell what to do?

If any answer is no, the design is not finished.

::: exercise
**Exercise 1.3 — Diagnose**

1. Find five posters or flyers around you — shop signs, church notices, event
   posters.
2. For each, answer the three-metre test.
3. Identify which of the five amateur habits each one commits.
4. Choose one and sketch a better version on paper.

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** five diagnoses and one sketch.
:::

# Module 2 — Colour That Works

## Lesson 2.1 — The three-colour rule

One dominant colour, one secondary, one accent. That is all. More than three
colours reads as noise, and noise is not remembered.

For a business: a dark colour for text and background structure, a bright colour
for emphasis, and a neutral for space.

## Lesson 2.2 — What colours do

| Colour | Signals | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Urgency, energy, warning | Sales, deadlines, calls to action |
| Blue | Trust, calm, professional | Finance, health, technology, education |
| Green | Growth, health, money | Agriculture, environment, savings |
| Gold / yellow | Value, warmth, attention | Premium, celebration, food |
| Purple | Creativity, luxury | Beauty, events, premium services |
| Black / white | Clarity, sophistication | Anything, as structure |

These are conventions, not laws, but they are conventions for a reason: they work
across cultures and they are what customers already expect.

## Lesson 2.3 — Contrast and accessibility

Text must be readable. Dark text on a light background, or light on dark — never
mid-tone on mid-tone.

A practical rule: if you have to squint, a customer will not read it. This matters
even more for older customers and for anyone reading on a cracked phone screen in
sunlight.

::: exercise
**Exercise 2.3 — Build a palette**

1. Choose three colours for your brand: dominant, secondary, accent.
2. Find their hex codes (Canva shows them).
3. Test readability: put your text colour on your background colour. Is it clear?
4. Make three versions of the same poster with different palettes.
5. Show them to five people. Ask which one they trust most.

**Time:** 40 minutes. **Deliverable:** a documented three-colour palette.
:::

# Module 3 — Typography

## Lesson 3.1 — Two fonts, maximum

One for headings, one for body text. If you must use one font, use it in two
weights. Never three.

Fonts have personality. A serif font (with the little feet) reads as traditional
and trustworthy. A sans-serif (clean, no feet) reads as modern and clear. A script
font reads as personal and is almost always a mistake for anything except a
signature or a wedding.

## Lesson 3.2 — Size and hierarchy

Three sizes, and that is enough:

- **Headline** — 3 to 5 times body size.
- **Subhead** — 1.5 to 2 times body size.
- **Body** — readable at arm's length.

## Lesson 3.3 — Spacing

Line spacing should be generous. Letters should not touch. Text should not touch
the edge of the design — leave a margin of at least 5 percent of the width.

Cramped text is the most common tell of an amateur layout, and the easiest to fix.

::: exercise
**Exercise 3.3 — The type exercise**

1. Take one short piece of text: your business name, a headline, and a line of
   detail.
2. Design it three ways: all one font; two fonts; two fonts with different
   weights.
3. Which reads most clearly at a distance?
4. Which feels most like the business you are describing?

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** three typographic studies.
:::

# Module 4 — Canva: The Working Method

## Lesson 4.1 — Templates, correctly used

A template is a starting point, not a finished design. The mistake beginners make
is leaving everything in place and only changing the words, which produces a
design that looks like the template rather than like the business.

The method: choose a template whose *structure* suits your purpose, then delete
everything you did not ask for, then rebuild the content.

## Lesson 4.2 — The Brand Kit

Set this up once and every future design is consistent:

- Logo (and a version with a transparent background)
- Two or three colours, with hex codes
- Two fonts
- A few reusable elements: a shape, a line, a badge

Brand Kit is the highest-value feature in Canva for a small business, and almost
nobody sets it up.

## Lesson 4.3 — Magic Resize

One design becomes every format in under a minute: Instagram post, story, WhatsApp
status, A5 flyer, Facebook cover.

This is the feature that changes the economics of design work. What used to be
four separate designs is now one design and three resizes.

## Lesson 4.4 — Export

| Use | Format | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Social media | PNG | Smaller file, sharp text |
| Photography-heavy | JPG | Smaller file |
| Print | PDF Print | Check bleed and resolution |
| Presentation | PDF Standard | Universal |

::: exercise
**Exercise 4.4 — Build the brand kit**

1. Create a Canva Brand Kit with logo, three colours, two fonts.
2. Design one poster using only those elements.
3. Magic Resize it to four formats.
4. Export each correctly.
5. Show the set to someone and ask whether they look like the same brand.

**Time:** 50 minutes. **Deliverable:** a brand kit and five consistent exports.
:::

# Module 5 — InShot: Short-Form Video

## Lesson 5.1 — Why video matters commercially

Video is the format that builds trust fastest, because it shows a real person, a
real place and a real product. A customer who has watched you explain something
has already begun to trust you.

## Lesson 5.2 — The InShot workflow

1. **Import** your clips.
2. **Trim** the start and end of each — remove the pause before you speak.
3. **Add text** — one line per idea, readable, on screen long enough to read.
4. **Add music** — from the app's library, at low volume.
5. **Export** at 1080p, in the ratio you need.

## Lesson 5.3 — The three-second rule

The first three seconds decide whether anyone watches the rest. Start with the
most interesting thing you have, not with a logo or a greeting.

## Lesson 5.4 — Captions

Most people watch with sound off. Add captions, and proofread them. A misspelled
price or a wrong name in a caption is permanent and public.

::: exercise
**Exercise 5.4 — A 30-second piece**

1. Film three short clips on your phone: a person, a product, a place.
2. Edit in InShot with text and music.
3. Add captions.
4. Export in vertical format.
5. Watch it with the sound off. Is it still clear?

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** one finished 30-second video.
:::

# Module 6 — AI in Design

## Lesson 6.1 — What has genuinely changed

Three years ago, a small business could not afford custom illustration,
background removal, or rapid iteration. Now all three are free or nearly free.

**Magic Media / text-to-image** — backgrounds, textures, illustrations, concepts.
**Background Remover** — product photos shot on a phone, cleaned up in seconds.
**Magic Eraser** — removing a distracting object.
**Magic Write** — copy inside the design.
**Magic Switch / Resize** — reformatting.

## Lesson 6.2 — What has not changed

Taste. Hierarchy. Knowing what the customer needs to see first. AI can generate
forty options; it cannot tell you which one is right for your audience. That
judgement is now the whole job, and it is why designers are not obsolete — they
are just faster.

## Lesson 6.3 — The honesty rules

::: warn
**Three rules for generated imagery.**

1. Never present a generated image as a photograph of your actual product or
   premises.
2. Never generate a person's likeness to imply endorsement.
3. Disclose when an image is generated, where a viewer would reasonably assume it
   is real.
:::

## Lesson 6.4 — The rejection habit

Generate six, keep one, delete five. Never use the first generation. This single
rule raises the quality of AI-assisted design more than any prompt technique.

::: exercise
**Exercise 6.4 — Product photo rescue**

1. Photograph a product on your phone, on any surface.
2. Remove the background in Canva.
3. Place it on a clean background colour from your palette.
4. Add a headline and a price.
5. Compare to the original photo. Measure the improvement honestly.

**Time:** 35 minutes. **Deliverable:** a cleaned product image ready to post.
:::

# Module 7 — Pricing and Selling Design Work

## Lesson 7.1 — What to charge

Price by the deliverable, not the hour, and know your floor.

A starting structure for a beginner in Uganda:

| Deliverable | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Social post (single) | 15,000–40,000 | Cheaper in sets |
| Set of 10 posts | 150,000–350,000 | Your best-value offer |
| Poster (A3, print-ready) | 50,000–120,000 | Includes two revisions |
| Logo | 150,000–500,000 | Includes three concepts |
| Short video (30s) | 100,000–300,000 | Includes captions |

These are starting points, not rules. The real determinant is what the client's
business gains, and what your time is worth to you.

## Lesson 7.2 — The scope conversation

Three sentences that prevent most disputes:

1. "This includes two rounds of changes."
2. "The final files are delivered in PNG and PDF."
3. "Additional rounds are charged at X per round."

Say them before you start. Every freelancer who has been asked for eleven
revisions wishes they had said sentence one.

## Lesson 7.3 — The portfolio

Six pieces, not sixty. Two showing range, two showing results, two in the style of
the client you want next. One page. A one-line description each.

::: exercise
**Exercise 7.3 — Package it**

1. Define three packages with prices.
2. Write your scope sentences.
3. Assemble six portfolio pieces.
4. Send the package to five potential clients.
5. Record what they ask — those questions are your next offer.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** a priced package and five sent messages.
:::

# Module 8 — Capstone: A Full Brand Set

## Lesson 8.1 — The brief

For a real or imagined business, produce: a logo, a three-colour palette, two
fonts, a social post template, a poster, a 30-second video, and a one-page
portfolio sheet.

## Lesson 8.2 — The rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consistency | 30% | Everything recognisably one brand | Each piece looks different |
| Hierarchy | 25% | Clear focal point, readable at distance | Cluttered, no focus |
| Craft | 20% | Aligned, spaced, correct exports | Crowded edges, blurry |
| Use of AI | 15% | AI used for elements, honestly | AI used to fake a product |
| Commercial readiness | 10% | Priced, scoped, presentable | Pretty but unsellable |
