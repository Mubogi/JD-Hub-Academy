# Module 1 — Why Presentations Fail

Almost every bad presentation fails for one of four reasons: too much text, no
structure, a presenter reading the slides, or no single point.

None of these are about software. They are about decisions made before the file is
opened. This course fixes the decisions first, then teaches the tool.

::: scripture
"A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver."
— Proverbs 25:11
:::

Fitly spoken. A presentation is a sequence of words chosen for a specific moment
and a specific listener. Not everything you know, arranged in order. The right
thing, said once.

## Lesson 1.1 — The one-sentence rule

Before building anything, write one sentence: **"After this presentation, my
audience will…"**

If you cannot finish that sentence, you are not ready to open PowerPoint. You are
ready to think.

## Lesson 1.2 — Slides support a speaker

A slide is not a document. It is a backdrop for a person talking. If the audience
is reading your slides, they are not listening to you, and you have become
redundant.

The test: could you delete the slide and still deliver the point? If yes, the
slide is decoration. If no, the slide is doing too much work.

## Lesson 1.3 — The structure that always works

| Section | Slides | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | 1 | The problem, or a question, or a number that surprises |
| **Frame** | 1 | What you will cover, and why it matters |
| **Body** | 3–7 | One point per slide, in logical order |
| **Close** | 1 | The ask, repeated clearly |
| **Appendix** | Any | Detail for questions, not for delivery |

Ten slides for a fifteen-minute talk. Twelve for twenty minutes. More than that
and you are not presenting, you are reading aloud.

::: exercise
**Exercise 1.3 — Plan on paper**

1. Write your one sentence.
2. Write the titles of your slides, one per line. No content yet.
3. Count them. If more than twelve, cut.
4. For each title, write the single point it makes.
5. Only then open PowerPoint.

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** a paper plan.
:::

# Module 2 — Slide Masters and Themes

## Lesson 2.1 — Why masters matter

The slide master controls every slide: fonts, colours, logo placement, title
position. Change it once, and the whole deck updates.

People who format each slide individually spend three times as long and produce an
inconsistent deck. This module fixes that permanently.

## Lesson 2.2 — Setting up the master

View → Slide Master.

Set:
- **Title font** — your brand heading font, size 36–44.
- **Body font** — your brand body font, size 20–28.
- **Colours** — from your brand palette, via the theme colours.
- **Logo** — once, on the master, not on each slide.
- **Footer** — slide number, organisation name.

Then close the master. Every new slide inherits all of it.

## Lesson 2.3 — The theme colours

PowerPoint themes have six accent colours. If you set these to your brand colours
once, every chart, table and shape will use them automatically.

That single setting is why some decks look designed and others look assembled.

## Lesson 2.4 — Layouts

The master includes layouts: Title Slide, Title and Content, Two Content,
Comparison, Blank. Use them. A consistent layout is the difference between a deck
that reads as one thing and one that reads as twenty.

::: exercise
**Exercise 2.4 — Build your master**

1. Open Slide Master.
2. Set your title and body fonts.
3. Set the theme colours to your brand palette.
4. Add your logo to the master, small, bottom right.
5. Close the master and add five slides. Check they all inherit correctly.

**Time:** 40 minutes. **Deliverable:** a reusable master.
:::

# Module 3 — Text, Hierarchy and Restraint

## Lesson 3.1 — The six-by-six guideline

Six bullets maximum, six words per bullet. Not a law, but a discipline that forces
you to decide what matters.

If you need more, you have two slides, not one crowded one.

## Lesson 3.2 — Titles that state a finding

| Weak title | Strong title |
|---|---|
| "Sales Results" | "Sales grew 22% after the price change" |
| "Challenges" | "Two branches are losing money" |
| "Next Steps" | "We need approval for 40 million by Friday" |

A title that states the finding means a person reading only the titles still gets
the argument. That is a well-built deck.

## Lesson 3.3 — Visual hierarchy

Three levels only: title, key point, supporting detail. Anything more and the eye
has nowhere to land.

Use size, weight and colour — not underlining, not WordArt, not shadows.

## Lesson 3.4 — White space

Empty space is what makes content legible. A slide with one line and a lot of space
is confident. A slide with twelve lines is frightened.

::: exercise
**Exercise 3.4 — Rewrite for restraint**

1. Take a text-heavy slide from any deck you have.
2. Reduce to six bullets of six words.
3. Rewrite the title to state the finding.
4. Increase the font size until it fills the space.
5. Compare the two versions.

**Time:** 25 minutes. **Deliverable:** a before-and-after slide.
:::

# Module 4 — Images, Icons and Shapes

## Lesson 4.1 — Images that earn their place

An image should either show something the audience could not otherwise see, or
create an emotional response that supports the point. Decorative stock photos of
people shaking hands do neither.

Rules: full-bleed images for impact, consistent treatment across the deck, and
never stretch an image out of proportion (hold Shift while resizing).

## Lesson 4.2 — Icons

Icons replace bullets effectively. Three icons in a row communicate "three things"
instantly, without reading.

Use one icon style throughout — all line icons, or all solid. Mixing styles is the
most common visual inconsistency in amateur decks.

## Lesson 4.3 — Shapes as structure

A rectangle behind text creates contrast and focus. A thin line separates sections.
A circle highlights a number.

Do not use shape effects — bevels, shadows, 3D. They date instantly and add nothing.

::: exercise
**Exercise 4.3 — Rebuild a slide visually**

1. Take a bulleted slide.
2. Replace the bullets with three icons and short labels.
3. Add a single image that supports the point.
4. Use one shape to create a focus area.
5. Compare to the original. Which is faster to understand?

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** a visually rebuilt slide.
:::

# Module 5 — Charts and Data

## Lesson 5.1 — One point per chart

The chart exists to make one comparison visible. If you cannot state the point in
the title, the chart is not ready.

## Lesson 5.2 — The settings that matter

- **Simplify.** Delete gridlines, legends, and axis lines you do not need.
- **Label the data directly.** A number on the bar is clearer than a legend.
- **Highlight one thing.** Grey out all the bars except the one you are discussing.
- **Round the numbers.** 1,247,392 becomes "1.2 million".
- **Set the y-axis to start at zero** for bar charts.

## Lesson 5.3 — Animation for data

Build a chart in stages: axes, then one series, then the second series. Each click
reveals one step. This is the one genuinely good use of animation — it controls
the pace of understanding.

::: exercise
**Exercise 5.3 — Rebuild a chart**

1. Insert a chart with your real data.
2. Delete gridlines and legend.
3. Add data labels.
4. Grey out everything except the most important bar.
5. Rewrite the title as a finding.

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** a chart that makes one point.
:::

# Module 6 — Animation and Transitions

## Lesson 6.1 — Transitions

One transition for the whole deck. Fade or Push. Set the duration to 0.5 seconds
and apply to all slides.

Anything more elaborate — spins, cubes, curtains — communicates that you have
discovered the feature, not that you have something to say.

## Lesson 6.2 — Animation, used well

Three legitimate uses:

1. **Reveal in sequence.** One bullet at a time, so the audience reads with you.
2. **Build a diagram.** Show the parts, then the whole.
3. **Focus attention.** Highlight the number you are discussing.

Three illegitimate uses: anything that bounces, anything that makes a sound, and
anything that makes the audience wait.

## Lesson 6.3 — The animation settings

Animations → Animation Pane. Set to "Start on Click" for content you control,
"After Previous" for things that should follow automatically.

Use "Appear" or "Fade". Not "Fly In" — it slows everything down and looks dated.

::: exercise
**Exercise 6.3 — Controlled reveal**

1. Take a slide with four bullets.
2. Apply Appear, on click, to each one.
3. Present it to one person, clicking deliberately.
4. Notice whether the pauses help or irritate.
5. Remove anything that irritated.

**Time:** 20 minutes. **Deliverable:** a controlled slide.
:::

# Module 7 — Presenter Tools and Rehearsal

## Lesson 7.1 — Presenter View

Slide Show → Presenter View. You see: the current slide, the next slide, your
notes, a timer, and the slide number.

This is the single most useful feature in PowerPoint, and most people have never
turned it on. On a projector, the audience sees only the slides; you see everything
else.

## Lesson 7.2 — Rehearse with timing

Slide Show → Rehearse with Coach (or Rehearse Timings). It records how long you
spend on each slide and tells you where you rushed.

The finding is almost always the same: you spent 60 percent of your time on the
first two slides and had to hurry the conclusion.

## Lesson 7.3 — Notes that help

Notes should be prompts, not a script. Three or four words per idea. If you write
the full text, you will read it, and reading is not presenting.

## Lesson 7.4 — The delivery habits

- Pause after your key point. Silence gives it weight.
- Look at one person per sentence.
- Do not read the slide. The audience can read.
- Have a printed backup. Technology fails.

::: exercise
**Exercise 7.4 — Rehearse properly**

1. Turn on Presenter View.
2. Rehearse with timing, three times.
3. Note where you went over.
4. Cut content from the slides where you went over.
5. Present to one person and ask: what was my main point?

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** a rehearsed deck and one honest answer.
:::

# Module 8 — Exporting and Sharing

## Lesson 8.1 — The formats

| Format | Use |
|---|---|
| **PPTX** | Editable, for people who will present |
| **PDF** | For sending, so nothing shifts |
| **Video (MP4)** | For self-running or social |
| **Images (PNG)** | For embedding in other documents |

## Lesson 8.2 — The handout

File → Print → "Handouts (3 slides per page)". Three slides and lines for notes.
This is what people actually read afterwards, and almost nobody produces it.

## Lesson 8.3 — Fonts and portability

If you use a font the presenting computer does not have, PowerPoint substitutes it
and your layout breaks. Either use standard fonts, or embed them (File → Options →
Save → Embed fonts), or present from PDF.

::: exercise
**Exercise 8.3 — Export all four**

1. Export your deck to PDF and check it on a phone.
2. Create a three-per-page handout.
3. Export to video.
4. Open the PPTX on a different computer. Does the layout hold?

**Time:** 25 minutes. **Deliverable:** four formats, checked.
:::

# Module 9 — AI-Assisted Slide Production

## Lesson 9.1 — What works

Generating an outline from a document, drafting speaker notes, rewriting bullets
shorter, suggesting a structure, and summarising a long report into ten slides.

## Lesson 9.2 — What fails

Generating a design that matches your brand, choosing which data matters, and
knowing your audience. Those remain yours.

## Lesson 9.3 — The workflow

1. Write your one sentence and your slide titles yourself.
2. Ask AI for speaker notes only.
3. Ask AI to cut every bullet to six words.
4. Build the design from your master.
5. Check every number against the source.

```prompt
Here are my slide titles. For each, write speaker notes of no more than 40
words, in a spoken register, that make the point without repeating the title.
Do not invent any figures.
```

::: exercise
**Exercise 9.3 — Notes with AI**

1. Take your deck titles.
2. Generate speaker notes with the prompt.
3. Rewrite every note in your own voice.
4. Cut each to 40 words or fewer.
5. Rehearse with the notes. Delete anything you did not say.

**Time:** 35 minutes. **Deliverable:** notes you actually use.
:::

# Module 10 — Capstone

## Lesson 10.1 — The brief

A ten-slide deck on a real topic, built on your own master, with one point per
slide, titles that state findings, one well-built chart, controlled animation, a
three-per-page handout, and a five-minute delivery.

## Lesson 10.2 — The rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | 25% | Clear argument, one point per slide | Random order, crowded |
| Restraint | 25% | Six-by-six, generous space | Paragraphs on slides |
| Visual craft | 20% | Consistent master, one icon style | Inconsistent, dated effects |
| Data | 15% | Chart makes one point, labelled | Unreadable chart |
| Delivery | 15% | Rehearsed, within time, no reading | Over time, reading aloud |

::: scripture
"Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned with salt, that you may know how
you ought to answer each one."
— Colossians 4:6
:::

Seasoned with salt — enough to be worth tasting, not so much that it overwhelms.
That is the whole craft of presenting.
