# Module 1 — How Money Moves Through a SACCO

A SACCO — a savings and credit co-operative — takes deposits from members and
lends those deposits back to members. That is the whole model. Everything else is
bookkeeping, governance, and risk control.

Understanding the model matters because most SACCO failures are not fraud. They
are a failure to understand that the money lent out belongs to other members, and
must come back.

::: scripture
"The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."
— Proverbs 22:7
:::

Servant to the lender. In a SACCO, the lender is not a bank in a distant city. It
is your neighbour's savings. That is a heavier responsibility than a commercial
lender carries, and it should shape every lending decision.

## Lesson 1.1 — The cycle

**Deposits in → loans out → repayments in → interest and dividends → back to
members.**

The cycle has one vulnerability: if more money goes out as loans than comes back
as repayments, the SACCO cannot pay a member who wants to withdraw. That is
called a liquidity crisis, and it is how SACCOs die.

## Lesson 1.2 — The four types of money in a SACCO

**Member savings** — withdrawable, owed to members, not the SACCO's money.
**Share capital** — the members' ownership stake, not withdrawable at will.
**Loan fund** — the pool available to lend.
**Reserves** — retained surplus, the buffer against losses.

Mixing these in your head is how a treasurer makes a decision that looks fine and
is catastrophic. They are not interchangeable.

## Lesson 1.3 — Why SACCOs exist

A commercial bank serves shareholders. A SACCO serves members. That difference
changes the lending decision: a SACCO can lend to someone a bank would decline,
because it knows them, and because the members collectively bear the risk.

That is the advantage and the danger in one sentence.

::: exercise
**Exercise 1.3 — Map your own SACCO**

1. Write down the total member savings, share capital, loan fund and reserves.
2. Calculate what percentage of savings is currently lent out.
3. If every member withdrew tomorrow, could the SACCO pay? (It cannot — no SACCO
   can. But how close is it?)
4. Write one sentence: what is the biggest risk in this balance?

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** a one-page position summary.
:::

# Module 2 — Double-Entry Bookkeeping

## Lesson 2.1 — The one idea

Every transaction affects two accounts, and the two must balance.

If a member deposits 100,000 cash: the cash account goes up by 100,000, and the
member's savings account goes up by 100,000. Two entries, one event.

This is not bureaucracy. It is a self-checking system: if the books do not
balance, something is wrong and you will know immediately.

## Lesson 2.2 — Debits and credits without the confusion

Forget the words for a moment. Use this instead:

**Where did the value go? Where did it come from?**

Cash received from a member: value went to the cash box, came from the member's
savings. Both recorded.

A loan disbursed: value went to the member, came from the loan fund. Both recorded.

## Lesson 2.3 — The five account types

| Type | Increases when | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets** (cash, loans receivable) | Value comes in | Debit |
| **Liabilities** (member savings) | Members deposit | Credit |
| **Equity** (share capital, reserves) | Surplus retained | Credit |
| **Income** (interest, fees) | Earned | Credit |
| **Expenses** (salaries, rent) | Incurred | Debit |

The rule that never fails: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity.** If it does not, there
is an error, and it must be found before anything else is done.

## Lesson 2.4 — The books every SACCO needs

- **Cash book** — every shilling in and out, daily.
- **Member ledger** — one page per member: deposits, withdrawals, loans, repayments.
- **Loan register** — every loan: amount, date, terms, balance, status.
- **General ledger** — the summary accounts.
- **Trial balance** — a monthly check that everything balances.

::: warn
**The most common bookkeeping failure in small SACCOs** is recording only the
cash movement and not the member's balance. The cash box matches, the member
records do not, and the discrepancy is discovered years later when a member
withdraws.
:::

::: exercise
**Exercise 2.4 — Record ten transactions**

1. Take ten real transactions from a week.
2. For each, write the two entries: where value went, where it came from.
3. Total both sides. They must be equal.
4. Produce a trial balance.
5. Find any transaction you cannot record — that is a process gap.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** ten double entries and a balanced trial
balance.
:::

# Module 3 — The Savings Cycle

## Lesson 3.1 — Why savings discipline is the whole foundation

A SACCO with weak savings discipline is a lending club with no capital. Members
must deposit consistently, and the SACCO must make depositing easy and
withdrawing slightly inconvenient — not to trap people, but because savings that
can be withdrawn at any moment are not savings.

## Lesson 3.2 — Products that work

| Product | Purpose | Design |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary savings** | Daily/weekly deposits, withdrawable | Low minimum, frequent deposits |
| **Fixed deposit** | Locked for a term, higher interest | Minimum amount, penalty for early exit |
| **Target savings** | Save toward a specific goal | Fixed term, fixed amount |
| **Share capital** | Ownership | Not withdrawable while a member |

## Lesson 3.3 — The withdrawal policy

Every SACCO needs a written rule answering: how much notice, what limits, what
happens in a liquidity squeeze.

The rule that protects everyone: withdrawals above a threshold require notice.
That single clause has saved many SACCOs from collapse during a rumour.

::: exercise
**Exercise 3.3 — Design a savings product**

1. Design one savings product for your members.
2. Specify: minimum deposit, frequency, interest, withdrawal rules, penalties.
3. Calculate what it costs the SACCO per year in interest.
4. Write the one-page member-facing description.
5. Test it on three members: would they use it?

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** a complete product specification.
:::

# Module 4 — The Loan Cycle

## Lesson 4.1 — The five stages

**Application → Appraisal → Approval → Disbursement → Repayment.**

Skipping appraisal is how SACCOs fail. It is also the stage most often rushed,
because the applicant is a friend, a relative, or a person of standing in the
community.

## Lesson 4.2 — Appraisal that actually works

Four questions, and the answers must be written down:

1. **Can they repay?** Income, verified, not asserted.
2. **Will they repay?** Character, evidenced by history — with this SACCO and
   others.
3. **What if they do not?** Collateral, guarantors, or a salary deduction.
4. **Is the purpose productive?** A loan for school fees is repaid from income; a
   loan for a funeral is repaid from nothing.

## Lesson 4.3 — The loan policy document

Every SACCO needs one, and it should fit on four pages:

- Eligible members and minimum saving history
- Maximum loan as a multiple of savings
- Interest rate and calculation method
- Repayment period and frequency
- Security required
- Appraisal requirements
- Approval authority by amount
- Penalties for late payment
- Write-off procedure

Without this document, every loan is a negotiation, and negotiations favour the
loudest person.

::: exercise
**Exercise 4.3 — Write the policy**

1. Draft the nine sections above for a SACCO you know.
2. Set a maximum loan multiple that protects liquidity.
3. Set approval authority: who can approve what amount.
4. Calculate: if all loans defaulted, what would members lose?
5. Have someone who was not involved read it and ask questions.

**Time:** 75 minutes. **Deliverable:** a four-page loan policy.
:::

# Module 5 — Portfolio Quality

## Lesson 5.1 — The numbers that matter

**Portfolio at Risk (PAR).** The percentage of the loan book that is overdue by
more than a set period, usually 30 days.

PAR = (outstanding balance of loans overdue 30+ days) ÷ (total loan book) × 100.

A PAR above 5 percent is a warning. Above 10 percent is a crisis. Above 20 percent
means the SACCO is technically insolvent and does not know it.

**Arrears rate.** Overdue amount ÷ total outstanding. Similar, but measures the
amount rather than the number of loans.

**Write-off ratio.** Loans written off ÷ total loans. Measures how much has
actually been lost.

## Lesson 5.2 — Ageing

Group every overdue loan by how long it is overdue:

| Age | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1–30 days | Early | Call today. Most recover here. |
| 31–90 days | Serious | Visit, restructure, involve guarantors |
| 91–180 days | Doubtful | Legal steps, collateral |
| 180+ days | Loss | Write off, recover separately |

Most recoveries happen in the first 30 days. A SACCO that only acts at 90 days
has already lost most of what it will lose.

## Lesson 5.3 — The collection discipline

Three habits: call on day one of arrears, record every contact, escalate on a
schedule rather than on emotion.

::: exercise
**Exercise 5.3 — Calculate and act**

1. Calculate your PAR at 30 days.
2. Build the ageing table for all overdue loans.
3. Identify the three largest 1–30 day arrears.
4. Contact all three today. Record what happened.
5. Repeat weekly for a month and compare.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** a PAR calculation and three contacts made.
:::

# Module 6 — Reporting to the Board

## Lesson 6.1 — What a board actually needs

Not every transaction. Six things:

1. **Membership** — number, growth, active vs dormant.
2. **Savings** — total, growth, withdrawal trend.
3. **Loan book** — total, number of loans, average size.
4. **Portfolio quality** — PAR, ageing, write-offs.
5. **Liquidity** — cash plus near-cash against likely withdrawals.
6. **Surplus** — income minus expenses, and the trend.

## Lesson 6.2 — The one-page dashboard

Six numbers, with a comparison to last month and to the same month last year.
Plus three sentences: what improved, what worsened, what the board must decide.

A board that receives twenty pages reads none of them. A board that receives one
page makes decisions.

## Lesson 6.3 — The questions a board should ask

Train your board to ask these five:

1. Is PAR rising, and why?
2. Are the largest borrowers still able to repay?
3. Can we meet withdrawals if ten members ask this week?
4. Is our interest income covering our costs and our risk?
5. What decision are we being asked to make today?

::: exercise
**Exercise 6.3 — Build the dashboard**

1. Build the six-number dashboard for your SACCO.
2. Fill in last month's figures.
3. Write the three sentences.
4. Present it in five minutes to someone acting as the board.
5. Answer their five questions.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** a one-page dashboard.
:::

# Module 7 — Regulation and Governance

## Lesson 7.1 — Why governance is not optional

A SACCO holds other people's money. Governance is the set of rules that stops
that money being lost through carelessness, conflict of interest, or theft.

The three essentials: separation of duties (the person who records is not the
person who holds cash), a board that meets and minutes decisions, and an annual
audit by someone independent.

## Lesson 7.2 — The regulatory basics in Uganda

SACCOs are regulated under the Tier 4 Microfinance Institutions and Money Lenders
Act, with supervision through the Uganda Microfinance Regulatory Authority. Key
obligations include registration and licensing, minimum governance standards,
record-keeping, and reporting.

::: warn
**Verify current requirements directly.** Regulation changes, and this course is
not legal advice. Confirm your obligations with UMRA or a qualified local
professional before relying on any summary, including this one.
:::

## Lesson 7.3 — The audit that protects you

An annual audit is not an accusation. It is protection: it proves to members that
the money is where the books say it is. A SACCO that resists audit is a SACCO
members should leave.

::: exercise
**Exercise 7.3 — Governance check**

1. List your board members and their roles.
2. Identify any conflict of interest — a board member who is also a large borrower.
3. Confirm separation of duties: who records, who holds cash, who approves.
4. Check whether your last audit was within twelve months.
5. Write one action for each gap.

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** a governance gap list with actions.
:::

# Module 8 — Using the JD Hub SACCO System

## Lesson 8.1 — What it does

The system records members, savings, loans, repayments and expenses, and produces
the reports in Module 6 automatically. It works offline, which matters where
connectivity is unreliable.

## Lesson 8.2 — Daily discipline

- Record every transaction the day it happens. Not weekly.
- Reconcile cash against the system every evening.
- Print or back up the day's records.
- Investigate any difference immediately. A difference found on day one is a
  mistake; found on day thirty, it is a mystery.

## Lesson 8.3 — Month-end

Run the trial balance, calculate PAR, produce the dashboard, back up the data, and
present to the board.

::: exercise
**Exercise 8.3 — Run a month**

1. Enter one full month of transactions into the system.
2. Reconcile cash daily for that month.
3. Run the trial balance. Does it balance?
4. Produce the dashboard.
5. Note any transaction you could not record properly.

**Time:** 90 minutes. **Deliverable:** a complete month, reconciled.
:::

# Module 9 — Capstone

## Lesson 9.1 — The brief

Produce, for a real or realistic SACCO: a loan policy, a savings product
specification, a PAR calculation with an ageing table, a one-page board dashboard,
and a governance gap list.

## Lesson 9.2 — The rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bookkeeping | 25% | Balanced, complete, reconciled | Cash only, no member ledger |
| Loan policy | 25% | Protects liquidity, clear authority | Vague, negotiable |
| Portfolio analysis | 25% | PAR calculated, ageing, actions | No measurement |
| Reporting | 15% | One page, decision-oriented | Data dump |
| Governance | 10% | Gaps identified with actions | Assumed fine |

::: scripture
"A just weight and balance are the Lord's; all the weights in the bag are His
work."
— Proverbs 16:11
:::

Honest scales. In a SACCO, the books are the scales, and the members are trusting
you to hold them level. That trust is the entire institution. Lose it once and no
amount of interest income will rebuild it.
