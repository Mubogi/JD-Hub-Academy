# Module 1 — The Phone Is the Studio

You do not need a camera, a light, a microphone or an editing suite. You need a
phone, a window, and a reason to press record. Every technique in this course is
designed for a handset that costs less than a single day's shoot with a
professional crew.

What separates content that works from content that does not is almost never the
equipment. It is whether the first three seconds earn the next three.

::: scripture
"A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver."
— Proverbs 25:11
:::

Fitly spoken. That is the whole craft — the right words, in the right setting,
at the right moment. Not more words. The right ones.

## Lesson 1.1 — What "good content" actually means

Four things, in this order. If any one is missing, the others do not save it.

**1. It is for someone specific.** "Everyone" is not an audience. "Parents of
primary-age children in Kampala who are choosing a holiday programme" is an
audience. The narrower the better, every time.

**2. It says one thing.** One idea per piece. The moment you add a second idea,
you have halved the impact of the first.

**3. It earns attention in three seconds.** The hook. A question, a surprising
number, a visible problem, a face looking at the camera. Not a logo, not an
introduction, not "hello everyone".

**4. It asks for something.** Watch, comment, message, come, buy. A piece with no
ask is decoration.

## Lesson 1.2 — The three content jobs

Everything you make should be doing one of three jobs. Know which one before you
start, because each requires a different structure.

| Job | Purpose | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attract** | Reach people who do not know you | Hook → value → soft ask | "Three things nobody tells you about…" |
| **Convince** | Move someone who is considering you | Problem → proof → offer | A customer story with a result |
| **Retain** | Keep people who already chose you | Value → belonging → ask | Behind-the-scenes, tips for existing customers |

Most small businesses make only "convince" content, aimed at people who are not
yet listening. That is why it feels like shouting.

## Lesson 1.3 — The kit you already own

- **Phone.** Shoot at 1080p, 30 frames per second. Not 4K — it fills your storage
  and slows your editing for no visible gain on social media.
- **Window.** The best free light you will ever have. Face the window, never sit
  with it behind you.
- **A wall.** Any plain wall is a studio backdrop.
- **Earphones with a microphone.** Dramatically better audio than the phone's
  built-in mic at any distance.
- **A stack of books.** Your tripod.

::: exercise
**Exercise 1.3 — The three-second test**

1. Open the last three videos or posts you published.
2. Watch only the first three seconds of each.
3. Write down, for each: what is happening, what is said, and whether you would
   keep watching if you did not know you had made it.
4. Be honest. Most people fail this exercise, and that is the point.

**Time:** 15 minutes. **Deliverable:** three honest notes.
:::

# Module 2 — Shooting Video That Looks Deliberate

The difference between amateur and professional video, on a phone, is five
decisions. None of them require money.

## Lesson 2.1 — Light

**Face the light.** If the window is behind you, you become a silhouette. This one
rule fixes most bad video.

**Avoid overhead midday sun.** It creates shadows under the eyes that make people
look tired.

**Use one light source, not three.** Mixed colour temperatures — window plus
fluorescent plus phone screen — is what makes footage look cheap.

**Golden hour is real.** The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset flatter
every skin tone and every street.

## Lesson 2.2 — Sound

Sound matters more than picture. Viewers forgive a soft image; they abandon bad
audio in two seconds.

**Get close.** Within one metre, or use the earphone mic.

**Kill the noise.** Turn off fans, fridges, the radio. Record away from traffic.

**Record ten seconds of room tone** at every location. You will need it in editing
to smooth cuts.

**Speak slightly slower than feels natural.** Nervousness speeds you up.

## Lesson 2.3 — Framing

**Rule of thirds.** Put the face on a third line, not in the centre. Turn on the
grid in your camera settings.

**Eye level.** Not looking up your nose, not looking down on you. Match the lens
to the subject's eye height.

**Headroom.** A little space above the head, not half the frame.

**Stability.** Rest the phone on something. Handheld drift reads as amateur even
when the content is good.

**Vertical for social, horizontal for YouTube and presentations.** Decide before
you shoot; cropping later loses quality.

## Lesson 2.4 — The shot list

Do not improvise. Write five shots before you start.

| Shot | Purpose | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Establishing** | Where are we | 3 s |
| **Presenter** | Who is speaking | 10–20 s |
| **Detail** | The product, the hands, the work | 3 s each |
| **Reaction** | A face responding | 2 s |
| **Call to action** | What to do next | 5 s |

Five shots, forty seconds of footage, is enough for a strong piece of content.

::: exercise
**Exercise 2.4 — Shoot the five shots**

1. Pick one subject: your product, your workspace, your service.
2. Shoot the five shots from the table. No retakes beyond two attempts each.
3. Watch it back with the sound off, then with sound only.
4. Note which decision — light, sound, framing, or stability — let you down most.

**Time:** 30 minutes. **Deliverable:** five clips and one honest diagnosis.
:::

# Module 3 — Scripting for Attention

## Lesson 3.1 — The hook, the body, the ask

Every short piece of content has three parts, and they get roughly 3 seconds, 20
seconds, and 5 seconds respectively.

**The hook** must create a reason to keep watching. Five hook shapes that work:

1. **The question they are already asking.** "Is it cheaper to repair or replace?"
2. **The surprising number.** "This cost me 12,000 shillings and saved me a week."
3. **The visible problem.** Show the broken thing, the mess, the queue.
4. **The contradiction.** "Everyone says to post more. I post less and sell more."
5. **The promise of a specific outcome.** "By the end of this you will know how to
   price a poster."

**The body** delivers one idea, with proof. Proof is a demonstration, a number, a
before-and-after, or a customer's words.

**The ask** is one sentence. One. Not three.

## Lesson 3.2 — Writing for the ear

Written text and spoken text are different crafts. Spoken text needs:

- Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
- Repetition of the key phrase, deliberately.
- Concrete nouns: "a 5,000 shilling note", not "a small amount".
- No subordinate clauses. If you need a comma to breathe, you needed a full stop.

Read every script aloud before recording. Anything you stumble over, rewrite. The
stumble is the audience's confusion, arriving early.

## Lesson 3.3 — The 30-second script template

::: box
**HOOK (0–3 s)**
[One sentence. The reason to keep watching.]

**SETUP (3–8 s)**
[Who this is for, and what they will get.]

**BODY (8–25 s)**
[One idea. One piece of proof. Demonstrated, not described.]

**ASK (25–30 s)**
[One action. Say it plainly.]

**ON-SCREEN TEXT**
[Six words maximum, reinforcing the hook. Not a transcript.]
:::

## Lesson 3.4 — Using AI to script without sounding like AI

AI is excellent at producing ten hook variants. It is poor at knowing which one
sounds like you. Use it for volume, then choose with your own ear.

```prompt
You are a scriptwriter for short social video. My audience is [specific
audience]. My topic is [one idea]. Write ten hook options, each under 12 words,
using a different one of these shapes: question, number, visible problem,
contradiction, promised outcome. No clichés like "in today's world".
```

Then:

```prompt
Take hook 4 and write a 30-second script using this structure: hook (1
sentence), setup (1 sentence), body (3 sentences with one concrete example),
ask (1 sentence). Write it to be spoken, not read. Maximum 80 words total.
```

Then the step everyone skips: **read it aloud and rewrite every phrase that is not
how you talk.** The AI gives you a scaffold; the voice has to be yours or the
audience will feel the absence of a person.

::: exercise
**Exercise 3.4 — Ten hooks, one script**

1. Write your one idea and your specific audience.
2. Generate ten hooks with the prompt.
3. Circle the one you would actually say to a friend.
4. Write the 30-second script around it.
5. Record it on your phone. Listen back. Rewrite anything that sounds like
   writing rather than speech.

**Time:** 40 minutes. **Deliverable:** one recorded 30-second piece.
:::

# Module 4 — Editing with CapCut

## Lesson 4.1 — The four-pass method

Do not edit in one pass. Do four, in this order. It is faster and the result is
better.

**Pass 1 — Assembly.** Put the clips in order. Nothing else. No effects, no music.

**Pass 2 — Trim.** Cut the first and last half-second of every clip, and every
pause longer than a breath. This pass alone makes footage feel professional.

**Pass 3 — Captions and text.** Auto-captions on, then proofread every word. Add
one on-screen text element per idea.

**Pass 4 — Sound and colour.** Music at low volume, one colour adjustment, export.

Most beginners start at pass 4. That is why their videos feel busy and slow at the
same time.

## Lesson 4.2 — Captions are not optional

Most people watch with sound off. Captions are the difference between being
understood and being scrolled past.

CapCut's auto-captions are accurate but not perfect. Proofread every line. A
caption error is public, permanent, and makes you look careless — especially when
it is a customer's name or a price.

## Lesson 4.3 — Music without legal risk

Using a song you did not license is a genuine risk: muted videos, takedowns, and
in some cases account penalties. Use the platform's built-in library or a
licensed source. The in-app libraries exist precisely so you do not need to think
about this.

::: warn
**Never use commercial music you have not licensed.** "Everyone does it" is not a
licence. A muted campaign video the week of your launch is an expensive lesson.
:::

## Lesson 4.4 — The export settings

| Platform | Ratio | Resolution | Length sweet spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| TikTok / Reels / Shorts | 9:16 | 1080 × 1920 | 15–35 s |
| Instagram feed | 4:5 | 1080 × 1350 | 20–60 s |
| Facebook feed | 1:1 or 4:5 | 1080 × 1080 | 30–90 s |
| YouTube | 16:9 | 1920 × 1080 | 3–10 min |
| WhatsApp status | 9:16 | 1080 × 1920 | under 30 s |

Export once per ratio. Do not crop a vertical video into a horizontal one and hope.

::: exercise
**Exercise 4.4 — Edit with the four passes**

1. Take the footage from Exercise 2.4.
2. Run all four passes, in order, without skipping.
3. Proofread every caption.
4. Export in two ratios.
5. Compare it to your last video. Write down the single biggest improvement.

**Time:** 45 minutes. **Deliverable:** one finished video in two formats.
:::

# Module 5 — AI in the Production Line

## Lesson 5.1 — Where AI genuinely helps

| Stage | AI does well | AI does badly |
|---|---|---|
| Ideas | Twenty angles in a minute | Knowing which fits your brand |
| Scripting | Structure, hook variants | Your actual voice |
| Voiceover | Explainer and internal video | Emotion, humour, local idiom |
| Images | Backgrounds, illustrations, patterns | Your actual product |
| Captions | Fast accurate transcription | Local names and slang |
| Editing | Auto-cut, auto-reframe | Taste |
| Repurposing | Transcript to clips to captions | Deciding what is worth repurposing |

## Lesson 5.2 — Synthetic voice, used honestly

Synthetic voice is now good. It is appropriate for: explainers, internal
training, audio versions of written content, and narration where no human voice
is available.

It is not appropriate for: pretending a real person said something, testimonial
content, or any situation where a listener would feel deceived on discovering the
voice was generated.

::: warn
**Consent rule.** Never clone or imitate a specific real person's voice without
their written permission. This is a legal matter in a growing number of
jurisdictions and a reputational matter everywhere.
:::

## Lesson 5.3 — Generated images in content

Generated images work well as: backgrounds, textures, conceptual illustrations,
thumbnails, and pattern elements.

They work badly as: photographs of your product, your premises, your team, or an
event that happened. Using them that way is misrepresentation, and customers
eventually find out.

## Lesson 5.4 — The repurposing chain

One recording, many assets. This is how a one-person media operation competes.

**Record 20 minutes → transcribe → pull 5 quotes → write 5 captions → cut 3
vertical clips → write 1 article → design 3 quote graphics.**

That is fourteen assets from one sitting. The work is in the selection, not the
production, and selection is where your judgement earns its place.

::: exercise
**Exercise 5.4 — One to fourteen**

1. Record 20 minutes on a topic you know well. No script.
2. Transcribe it.
3. Produce: 5 captions, 3 clips, 1 written piece, 3 graphics.
4. Publish at least five of the assets over one week.
5. Record which format performed best.

**Time:** 90 minutes. **Deliverable:** at least five published assets from one
recording.
:::

# Module 6 — Building a Series People Return To

## Lesson 6.1 — Why series beat one-offs

A single good post is a lottery ticket. A series is an asset. People return for
the next episode, which means you stop paying for attention every time.

A series needs three things: a repeatable format, a predictable schedule, and a
reason to come back.

## Lesson 6.2 — Formats that survive

| Format | Structure | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The weekly tip** | One problem, one fix, 45 seconds | Short, useful, repeatable |
| **Behind the counter** | Show how something is made | Trust through transparency |
| **Customer question** | Real question, real answer | Infinite supply of topics |
| **Myth vs fact** | Common belief, correction | High share rate |
| **The countdown** | "Three things to check before…" | Easy to consume, easy to save |
| **Progress log** | Show a project over weeks | Builds investment |

Pick one. Do it twelve times before judging it. Most people quit at four, which
is exactly when the audience starts to recognise the format.

## Lesson 6.3 — The content bank

Keep a running document of every question a customer has ever asked you. That
document is your content plan for the next two years, and it is written by your
customers, which means it is guaranteed relevant.

::: exercise
**Exercise 6.3 — Launch a series**

1. Choose one format from the table.
2. Write the format rules: length, structure, publishing day, opening line.
3. Produce three episodes.
4. Publish them on schedule.
5. Write the date of episode twelve, and commit to it.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** three published episodes and a calendar.
:::

# Module 7 — Publishing and Measuring

## Lesson 7.1 — The minimum viable publishing system

You need four things and nothing more:

1. One folder per week for assets.
2. One scheduler (Meta Business Suite or Canva Content Planner).
3. One spreadsheet with three columns: date, format, result.
4. One 15-minute review each Friday.

Anything more elaborate becomes a second job and gets abandoned.

## Lesson 7.2 — The numbers that matter

Ignore follower count. It is the least useful number available.

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **3-second retention** | Whether your hook works |
| **Completion rate** | Whether the body holds attention |
| **Saves** | Whether it was useful enough to keep |
| **Shares** | Whether it was worth passing on |
| **Profile visits → messages** | Whether it produces customers |

Track saves and shares above likes. Likes are politeness; saves are intent.

## Lesson 7.3 — The honest review

Every Friday, three questions:

1. Which piece got the most saves, and why?
2. Which piece got the fewest, and why?
3. What will I do differently next week — one change only?

One change per week. More than that and you learn nothing, because you cannot
tell what worked.

::: exercise
**Exercise 7.3 — Build the system**

1. Create the four-part system above.
2. Log everything you publish for four weeks.
3. Do the Friday review every week without exception.
4. At the end, write one page: what worked, what did not, what you will stop
   doing.

**Time:** 15 minutes per week for four weeks.
**Deliverable:** one page of evidence-based conclusions.
:::

# Module 8 — Making It Pay

## Lesson 8.1 — The three routes to income

Content does not pay you. Content creates the conditions for one of three things:

**Sell your own product or service.** The most direct. Every piece ends with a
path to purchase.

**Sell content work to others.** Once you can produce consistently, businesses
will pay you to do it for them. This is the fastest route for a skilled beginner.

**Build an audience you can serve.** Slowest, largest, and least reliable in the
short term. Real, but do not start here if you need income this quarter.

## Lesson 8.2 — Pricing content work

Do not price by the hour. Price by the deliverable, and know your floor.

A simple structure that works for a beginner:

| Package | Contents | Basis for price |
|---|---|---|
| **Starter** | 4 posts + 1 video per month | Your time × 3 |
| **Standard** | 8 posts + 4 videos + captions | Your time × 3 |
| **Full** | Everything plus strategy and reporting | Your time × 4, plus value |

The multiplier is not greed. It covers revision rounds, client communication,
tool costs, and the unbillable time every freelancer forgets.

## Lesson 8.3 — The portfolio that sells

You need six pieces, not sixty.

- Two showing range of style.
- Two showing results (with numbers if you have them).
- Two in the style of the client you want next.

Put them on one page, with a one-line description each and what you did. Send
that page. Nobody reads a fifty-piece gallery.

::: scripture
"Do you see a man who excels in his work? He will stand before kings; he will not
stand before unknown men."
— Proverbs 22:29
:::

Excellence in the work is the marketing. The portfolio is only evidence of it.

::: exercise
**Exercise 8.3 — Package and price**

1. Write your three packages with prices.
2. Build the six-piece portfolio page.
3. Identify five businesses that need exactly what you now do.
4. Send the portfolio to all five with a one-paragraph message.
5. Record the responses.

**Time:** 60 minutes. **Deliverable:** a priced offer and five sent messages.
:::

# Module 9 — Capstone: A Content Operation

## Lesson 9.1 — The brief

Run a complete content operation for four weeks: one series, twelve published
pieces, a measured result, and a portfolio of the work.

## Lesson 9.2 — The rubric

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook craft | 25% | Three-second retention clearly above your baseline | Starts with a logo or greeting |
| Consistency | 25% | Twelve pieces, on schedule | Bursts and silence |
| Measurement | 20% | Saves and shares tracked, conclusions drawn | Likes only |
| Craft | 20% | Clean audio, readable captions, deliberate framing | Shaky, quiet, uncaptioned |
| Outcome | 10% | Enquiries or sales attributed | No ask, no result |

**A closing reflection**

Lord, let what I make be true, useful, and kind. Let me not chase attention for
its own sake, but earn it honestly and spend it well. Guard me from the temptation
to make something look better than it is.
Amen.
:::
